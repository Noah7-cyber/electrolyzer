Theoretical Models & Core Concepts

Electrolysis is the process of using an electrical current to drive a non-spontaneous chemical reaction. In an aqueous solution, water (H₂O) is split into its constituent elements: hydrogen gas (H₂) at the cathode, and oxygen gas (O₂) at the anode.

2H₂O(l) → 2H₂(g) + O₂(g)

To maintain conductivity, an electrolyte (such as KOH or NaOH) is added to the distilled water, creating a path for ion exchange without being consumed in the primary reaction.

Faraday's First Law states that the mass of a substance altered at an electrode is directly proportional to the total electrical charge transferred. For engineering purposes, we map total amperage to gas yield.

V = ( I × t × Vₘ ) / ( z × F )

In this dashboard's simulation, this is simplified mathematically to a direct yield constant based on assumed ideal efficiency, penalized by thermal losses and internal resistance.

The minimum voltage required to split water is not constant. The Nernst equation demonstrates how the reversible cell voltage decreases slightly as the system's ambient temperature rises.

E = E° - ( R × T ) / ( z × F ) × ln(Q)

While increasing temperature lowers the required voltage threshold (aiding efficiency), excessive heat represents wasted power. Unused wattage converts to thermal energy, heating the fluid according to its specific heat capacity and eventually leading to boiling off the water level.

Achieving electrolysis requires overcoming not just the thermodynamic threshold, but also kinetic barriers at the electrode surfaces. This extra required voltage is known as activation overpotential. The Tafel equation models this resistance at higher current densities.

η = a × log₁₀( i / i₀ )

Material selection is crucial here. Platinum has extremely high exchange current density (low barrier), while graphite or pencil lead requires far more power to drive the same reaction rate. As current density scales, the overpotential penalty rapidly increases.
